Where Round Comes From

Runway, step 1b — a dissection. "Round" is not a shape you start with. It is what uniform reach looks like once you decide which moves are allowed. Change the moves and the round thing changes; make one direction special and it disappears.

Reach

Stand on one cell of a grid. Pick a rule for moves. Colour every cell you can reach with a budget of n moves. The coloured region is your reach; its edge is everything exactly n moves away — "equally far" in the only sense the rule knows.

What you just saw

Four directions give a diamond. Eight give a square. Sixteen give a shape with more corners, closer to round. Any direction at all gives the round one. Nothing round was drawn in advance — the round thing is what the edge of "equally far" turns into as the rule stops preferring any direction. Round is the projection of uniformity.

And the last rule — left costs double — is the falsifier. Every direction is still allowed; only one is made special. The reach goes lopsided and there is no round thing to be found. Roundness needed uniformity, not the other way round.

The same dissection by dimension

Step 1's band lives in the first picture: on a line that loops, "equally far both ways" is two points, and the hopper could never see roundness because there was none to see. Round needs at least two directions to be uniform across.

Which of these is the real circle?

None of them, and all of them. Each is "equally far under its rule." The diamond is the circle of a city grid; the square is the circle of a chessboard king; the round one is the circle of open ground. Asking which is real is asking which rule is real — and the rule is a choice about moves, not a fact about shape. What is not a choice: whichever rule you pick, if it treats all directions alike, the reach has a centre, a single size, and looks the same turned any way. Those are the things "round" actually means. The smooth curve is the one case people draw first, which is why step 1 had to take it away.
