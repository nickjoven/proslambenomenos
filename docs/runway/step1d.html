What the three rules give

Six directions: an octahedron — a diamond in every slice, shrinking to a point at the top and bottom. Twenty-six: a cube — the same square in every slice. Any direction: the ball — round slices that shrink. Three dimensions did not add a new idea; it added a new axis and the same dissection ran through it.

Now the shadow. The octahedron's shadow is a diamond; the cube's is a square; the ball's is a round disc. The shadow of the 3-D reach is exactly the 2-D reach from step 1b. Looking at the floor alone, you cannot tell whether the thing casting it had a third axis. That's the hidden-axis lesson from step 1c, now with a spatial axis doing the hiding: a shadow keeps the symmetries of the floor and throws away every axis it flattened.

The one question

You are shown only the diamond shadow. Someone says "that's a two-dimensional reach with four moves." Can you tell?

No — and the slices are how you would find out. A shadow is one projection; the slices are a different one, and an octahedron's slices shrink while a flat diamond has only one slice. Two projections that disagree reveal the axis a single projection hid. Step 1 made this point with a track (one projection: how far round); step 1c with a car (the heading axis); here it is with up-and-down. It is the same point each time: one projection never settles what the thing is; two can.

The next honest axis to add is amplitude — a move that makes a thing bigger or smaller and can go negative — and that is where step 2's half turn ("comes back flipped") turns out to live.

Where it could break

Which sentence, which control, which picture. Notes so far: round smuggles in a pull; shapes that project to the same thing; dissect round out of uniformity; add axes by adding moves; now a real third axis and its shadow.
