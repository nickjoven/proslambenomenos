The Half Turn

Step 1 said round means coming back. This page is about the things that come back wrong — upside down — and only come back right if you go around twice. That one idea is the doorway to amplitudes, interference, and the half-steps your superconductor reading keeps running into.

1 · Arrows that add

Forget numbers. The object of this page is an arrow: it has a length and a direction, nothing else. Two arrows add head-to-tail. Drag the second arrow's direction and watch what the sum does.

The important case is the last one: two arrows of equal length, pointing opposite ways, add to nothing. Numbers that size can never cancel like this; arrows can. Hold that.

2 · The quarter turn is a move

Step 1c said an axis is something you do. Here is the only move this page needs: turn the arrow a quarter to the left. Do it repeatedly and count.

no turns yet — the arrow points right

Two quarter turns make a flip — the half turn, the arrow that cancels its old self. Four make a round. In step 1's language: the quarter turn is a move whose round is four. Everything this page does next is built from that one fact.

3 · Squares make chances

Here is the one rule connecting arrows to the world: the chance of a thing is the square built on its arrow — the arrow's length, times itself. When a thing can happen two ways, you do not add the chances. You add the arrows, then square.

Slide until the two ways point opposite: the square vanishes — a thing that can happen two ways can thereby happen less, even not at all. That is interference, and it is why the half turn matters: a half turn between two ways is the difference between bright and dark.

4 · The loop that flips you

Now the payoff. Walk a loop while carrying an arrow. On an ordinary loop, one lap brings the arrow home. But some loops have a twist in them — cross the marked seam and your arrow takes a half turn. Drag the walker around and watch the carried arrow.

drag the walker around the ring

One lap: the arrow returns flipped — the loop is round (you came back!) but the arrow's round is two laps. Step 1 defined round as coming back; this loop splits the definition in half: the place comes back in one lap, the arrow in two. Things that live on this loop come in halves.

The bridge to your reading. In a superconducting ring, the current carries exactly such an arrow (its phase), and "the arrow must come home" is why the trapped flux comes in whole steps — the winding integer. Put one π junction in the ring — one seam that half-turns the arrow — and the steps shift by exactly one half: the half flux quantum, the semifluxon. Same mechanics you just dragged. The two-ways-cancel rule of section 3 is the amplitude story; the seam of section 4 is the π ring this project measured (the graph's ring node; the folds and slips of P-35/P-36 are what it costs to change laps). One caution the project computed the hard way: not every ½ is this ½ — the flipping-loop half is the label of a state that exists; there are other halves (the all-or-nothing kind) that are the absence of one. Different animals, same numeral.

Where does this break?

Same question as the earlier steps, one page later: which section stops earning its keep first — the arrows, the squares, or the seam? Step 3 (why squares, of all rules) is the natural next door; it opens onto why the world adds arrows at all.
